# The Quiet Geometry of Noticing

It’s late July, and the light does that specific mid-summer thing where it hangs around too long, stretching out the afternoon until you feel like you’ve lived three different lives in the span of twelve hours. Yesterday, I found myself sitting on my kitchen floor, surrounded by mismatched plastic containers and half-empty bottles of cleaning spray. I was supposed to be organizing the cabinet under the sink—a task I had avoided for months—but instead, I found an old bottle of lavender dish soap I haven't used in years. I popped the cap and took a breath. Instantly, I was transported back to a tiny, drafty apartment I lived in four years ago, where the radiator hissed like an angry cat and the kitchen window looked out onto a brick wall. It is wild how a single scent can pull you through a wormhole of time, forcing you to confront the ghost of who you used to be.

It made me think about how much of our lives we carry around without realizing it. We are, all of us, walking collages of everyone we have ever loved, or even just tolerated for a season. I still chop garlic the way an old roommate taught me, even though we haven't spoken since college. I still use a specific brand of black gel pens because a quiet girl in a creative writing seminar lent me one once and it felt right in my hand. We are constantly absorbing these tiny, microscopic fragments of other people, stitching them into our own skin, and calling it a personality. It’s a beautiful thought, really. We are never truly starting over; we are just carrying the best and sometimes the weirdest parts of our past forward with us.

There is a strange, quiet comfort in realizing how fluid we are. When we are in the middle of a difficult chapter, we tend to view our lives as a fixed state. We think, this is how it is now. This is who I am, and this is how I will always feel. But the truth is, we are always under construction. The version of you that exists today is not the version that will exist next summer, or even next month. We are constantly shedding skin, leaving behind old preferences, old anxieties, and old ways of looking at the world. The things that used to keep you awake at night at nineteen are probably laughable to you now. The things that feel like insurmountable mountains today will eventually become hills you look back on from a distance, marveling at how easy they are to climb.

I spent an hour at a local coffee shop earlier this week, just watching the room move. I wasn't scrolling on my phone or reading a book; I was just practicing the increasingly rare art of looking. I watched a man in a crisp business suit carefully untie his leather shoes just to shake out a microscopic speck of dirt, his face entirely serious, as if that tiny pebble was the most pressing issue in the universe. I watched two teenagers share a single pair of wired headphones, sitting in a silence so comfortable it felt sacred, their heads tilted toward each other like flowers reaching for the sun. There is so much tenderness in the world if you actively look for it in the margins. We spend so much of our time looking for the grand gestures—the big promotions, the dramatic declarations of love, the monumental life shifts—that we miss the quiet, daily poetry of being alive.

Memory, of course, is a highly selective editor. It cuts out the boring parts—the traffic, the waiting in line, the quiet dinners where neither person had anything to say—and leaves behind this idealized, golden-hour version of the past. We look back at our earlier years and we think, god, we were so happy then. But we forget that we were also incredibly anxious, broke, and entirely unsure of who we were. We romanticize the past because it is safe; we know how the story ends. The present is terrifying because the pages are still blank, and we have to write them in real-time, without a script, in ink that cannot be erased.

Lately, I have been thinking a lot about the concept of softening. When you are younger, you want to be sharp. You want to have edges, to be defined, to be someone recognizable. You want your opinions to be loud and your aesthetic to be flawless. But as I get older, I find myself wanting to be soft. I want to be easy to talk to. I want to be the kind of person who notices when the afternoon light hits the living room wall at exactly 4:00 PM and pauses to appreciate it. I want to be forgiving of my own mistakes, and by extension, the mistakes of others. Softness isn't weakness; it's a form of resilience. It's the ability to absorb the bumps in the road without shattering into a million pieces.

We are always waiting for the next thing. We tell ourselves, I'll be happy when I get that job, when I move to that city, when I find that relationship, when the weekend finally arrives. But life is what happens in the waiting rooms. It's the lukewarm coffee, the bad radio station playing in the grocery store, the quiet drive home when the sun is setting and you don't feel like talking. If we spend all our time looking at the horizon, we miss the wildflowers growing right at our feet. The extraordinary is almost always hidden inside the ordinary.

So tonight, as the summer breeze moves the curtains in my bedroom and the world outside slowly quietens, I am trying to practice the art of noticing. I am noticing the quiet hum of the refrigerator. I am noticing the cool touch of the bedsheets. I am noticing the small, persistent hope that lives in my chest, even on the days when the world feels heavy and complicated. We don't need to have it all figured out today, or tomorrow, or even next year. We just need to keep showing up, keeping our eyes open, and letting ourselves be gently changed by the beautiful, messy, ordinary experience of being here.
